A price floor for soybeans with puts

A producer with soybeans to sell after harvest wants a minimum price without giving up a rally. Buying put options on the futures does that: below the strike the put makes up the fall, and above it the crop sells at the market. This guide works through one case with the calculator, from the put’s cost to the net price a ton.

The position

  • Soybean futures (CBOT ZS) at 1,050 ¢/bu, an illustrative price.
  • A put struck at 1,000 ¢, expiring in 60 days, American.
  • Volatility 18%, risk-free rate 4%.
  • The soybeans to sell as an underlying leg, long.
  • 5,000 metric tons, which is 36.74 contracts of 5,000 bushels; the scenario holds 37.

What the put costs

The calculator values the put at 11.200 ¢ a bushel: $560.02 a contract, $20,720.71 for the 37. That is the price of the floor, paid today.

The floor

Below 1,000 ¢ the put pays the difference, so the least the sale comes to is the strike less the premium. With a local basis of −40 ¢ to the futures and 1 ¢ of commissions, the net sale price cannot fall below 947.726 ¢ a bushel, 348.23 dollars a metric ton. Read nominally, as extension worksheets do, it is 1,000 − 40 − 11.200 − 1 = 947.800 ¢, 348.26 USD/t; the calculator also counts the interest the premium would have earned over the 60 days at 4%, which takes 0.074 ¢ off.

Above 1,000 ¢ the put expires worthless and the soybeans sell at the market: there is no ceiling.

What to watch

  • The basis is held fixed. In the market it moves, and a weaker basis lowers the floor point for point.
  • The put expires before the future: a December option stops trading in late November. Picking a listed month in the calculator sets the days to the option’s own expiry.
  • The 18% is an input, not the market’s implied volatility. A higher one makes the put dearer and the floor lower; the IV button reads it from a premium on your screen.

The prices in this guide are illustrative, not market data, and its figures are the calculator’s for them. Nothing here is investment advice.
